01 / Approach

How I work, when nobody's watching

Solve it once, then make it serve everyone

A connector fix for one customer became a maintained fork covering six runtimes that other teams now depend on. I look for the version of a fix that outlives the engagement, because the second customer with the same problem is always already out there.

Prove it before you ship it

I don't trust an optimisation measured on dev data, or a migration validated by sampling ten percent of rows. Two of my projects exist specifically because "probably fine" is not good enough when the blast radius is production.

Make safety structural, not behavioural

A sandbox that relies on people being careful will eventually meet somebody in a hurry. Fail-closed identity boundaries mean a stray redirect hits a permission wall instead of a production table — the guarantee holds even when the operator is wrong.

Leave it so someone else can own it

CI matrices, test suites, runbooks, and evidence stakeholders can validate without me in the room. The finish line is not deployment; it's the day the work no longer needs me.

Databricks · Enterprise customer · Cost engineering

Took $180,000 a year out of a customer's Databricks bill

Their platform spend was concentrated in a handful of production jobs. The brief was to reduce it without changing a single business output — no scope cuts, no dropped tables, same numbers at the end.

$180KVerified annual saving
50%Cut on the costliest job
4–5Of the top 20 most expensive jobs
0Business outputs changed

Problem

Spend followed the usual shape — a long tail of cheap jobs and a short head of very expensive ones. The costly jobs were also the load-bearing ones, which is exactly why nobody had touched them. Any change had to be provably output-neutral before finance would count the saving.

Approach

Profile before touching anything, and make every change independently verifiable. I documented each optimisation with the evidence behind it so stakeholders could validate the result themselves rather than take my word for it — which is what turned these into verified savings rather than claimed ones.

Approach in detail

  1. 1
    Rank by spend, not by complaint

    Worked from the top-20 most expensive jobs rather than the ones people were grumbling about. The two lists overlap less than you would expect.

  2. 2
    Rewrite the query logic

    Inefficient joins and redundant scans, targeted at the plan rather than the syntax.

  3. 3
    Fix the partitioning

    Data layout was doing more damage than the SQL in several cases — partitioning that made sense when the table was small and stopped making sense years ago.

  4. 4
    Remove work, not just speed it up

    Streamlined transformations to cut shuffle and drop redundant stages entirely. The cheapest stage is the one that no longer runs.

  5. 5
    Document so others can verify

    Every change shipped with before-and-after evidence, so the saving was validated by the customer rather than asserted by me.

What changed

Three jobs verified at $132,000/yr (50% reduction), $43,000/yr (30%) and $5,000/yr (10%) — $180,000 a year in total, with two further jobs still in verification. Business output was unchanged throughout. This is the piece of my work that translates most directly: deep Spark and Delta performance engineering, expressed as a number that appears on someone's budget.

Databricks · Open-source contribution · Lakebridge

Proved two billion-row tables match without moving the data

Every enterprise migrating onto Databricks has to prove the target matches the source. Row-by-row validation shuffles terabytes across the network, so at real scale teams give up, sample ten percent, and accept ninety percent confidence instead of proof.

8.1×Faster on real 74M-row data
~48,000×Less data across the wire
15.8BRows validated
0False positives or negatives

Problem

A 74-million-row table took around 506 seconds to validate the traditional way. Billion-row tables were effectively unvalidatable — hours to days, or simply infeasible. So the industry norm became sampling, which cannot prove equality; it can only fail to disprove it. Every migration customer hits this.

Approach

Stop comparing rows and start comparing mathematics. If you compute compact algebraic aggregates where the data already lives, you can compare fingerprints instead of payloads — and when they differ, recover the exact differing rows algebraically rather than by searching for them.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Hash and partition

    Each row hashed to a 64-bit integer with a cross-engine MD5 so source and target agree byte for byte, then bucketed into roughly a million sub-buckets.

  2. 2
    Fingerprint each bucket

    Five algebraic signals per bucket — count, sum of hashes, sum of squares, plus an independent second channel for collision detection. Source aggregates computed via JDBC pushdown, so about 50 KB crosses the wire instead of 2.4 GB of rows.

  3. 3
    Compare and short-circuit

    Broadcast-join the aggregates; matching buckets resolve instantly and are never looked at again. On a clean migration almost everything short-circuits here.

  4. 4
    Solve for the differences

    A single-pass algebraic solver recovers the exact differing row hashes from power-sum deltas — one difference resolves directly in about 97% of cases, two via a quadratic, and anything denser falls back to a surgical fetch. This is where it departs from the recursive bisection everyone else uses.

What changed

8.1× end to end on a real 74-million-row Redshift table (506s to 62s), 54.6× faster on the source side, and about 48,000× less data transferred. Validated up to 15.8 billion rows across 28 columns, with zero false positives, zero false negatives, and a 98.8%+ exact-difference solve rate. Contributed into Databricks' open-source Lakebridge migration tool behind an opt-in, fail-open flag — so existing users are unaffected and any team can turn it on with a single config change. Along the way I found and fixed three latent Redshift serialisation bugs that had been silently corrupting every Redshift reconcile.

Databricks · Platform tooling · Optimisation safety

Proved a 10× speedup against 4.45 billion rows, with production untouched

A Photon enablement looked fine in dev, ran three times slower in production, breached downstream SLAs and had to be reverted. Dev datasets are too small to show the skew and shuffle behaviour that actually decides whether an optimisation pays off.

10.19×Speedup proven safe
4.45BRows verified identical
21Output tables, all matching
30+Pipeline families inherited it

Problem

You cannot trust an optimisation measured on dev data, and you cannot test it in production without risking corruption and SLA breaches. So teams either ship on faith or never ship at all. There was no systematic way to demonstrate that output data stayed byte-identical while performance improved.

Approach

Safety had to be structural rather than behavioural. A sandbox that depends on people being careful will eventually meet somebody in a hurry, so I designed it so that a stray redirect hits a permission wall instead of a production table — the boundary holds even when the operator makes a mistake.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Discover the lineage

    Reads the catalog's own lineage tables rather than parsing code, unioning a 30-day lookback — and fails loudly on empty lineage instead of quietly cloning nothing.

  2. 2
    Gate on identity, fail closed

    Verifies the sandbox principal's effective group-inherited grants and pins the run identity. This is the load-bearing safety property: everything downstream can be wrong and production is still unreachable.

  3. 3
    Shallow-clone the inputs

    Metadata-only Delta clones give production-scale data with zero storage duplication — the reason testing at real volume is affordable at all.

  4. 4
    Redirect and run isolated

    Job parameters patched dynamically, no YAML edits, and unredirectable task types refuse to run rather than silently pointing at production.

  5. 5
    Emit a verdict

    Bidirectional EXCEPT ALL proves multiset parity, catching added, dropped and duplicated rows plus schema drift. Runtime is measured on execution duration to exclude cluster-start noise, and cost analysis is deferred to its own step because billing data lands hours later — it never prints a misleading $0.

What changed

On a real production pipeline: 301.5 minutes to 72.7 after a SQL rewrite (4.15×), then 29.6 minutes with Photon — 10.19× overall, at lower DBU than baseline. All 21 output tables identical across 4,450,457,930 rows, proven by bidirectional comparison rather than spot checks. Two engines — one generic, one wired into a shared catalog resolver — so a single backward-compatible change meant 30+ pipeline families inherited it with no per-pipeline work.

Databricks · Enterprise customer · Spark connector development

A fix for one customer that ended up serving everyone

Microsoft archived the Spark SQL Server connector. The customer needed a newer Databricks runtime for their Unity Catalog migration, and the moment they upgraded, every write failed. There was no official migration path and no precedent to follow.

~15×Write throughput vs generic JDBC
6Runtime versions covered
1Codebase for all of them
0Private Spark APIs relied on

Problem

The connector gave roughly 15× the bulk-upload throughput of generic JDBC, so it was load-bearing. Microsoft archived it in February. Upgrading to the runtime required for Unity Catalog broke an internal API signature and every write died on NoSuchMethodError. The migration stopped dead.

Approach

Root-cause it properly, then fix it so it cannot break the same way again. The interesting finding: the newer runtime forward-ports the next Spark major version's method signature while still reporting itself as the older version — so version detection was lying, and anything built on private APIs was going to keep breaking.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Trace the classpath

    Investigated across six runtime versions to find where the signature actually diverged, rather than trusting the reported version string.

  2. 2
    Ship the unblocking fix

    A custom schema-resolution method compatible with both Spark 3.5 and 4.0, plus a reworked build with multiple Maven profiles so it could be built locally for either Scala version.

  3. 3
    Delegate to the public API

    The durable version: a thin compatibility layer of roughly 250 lines, reimplemented entirely against Spark's stable public interfaces. No private types, and therefore no per-version source branching — future upgrades become mechanical CI additions instead of risky refactors.

  4. 4
    Make it maintainable by anyone

    Weekly runtime smoke tests to catch classpath drift, real SQL Server integration tests via containers, and a per-release million-row throughput gate that fails the build on more than 20% regression.

What changed

The Unity Catalog migration was unblocked and the customer confirmed everything working. It became the only known workaround for the problem, and spread on its own — colleagues asked for the JAR to unblock their own customers across multiple accounts. I then productised it into a maintained fork covering six runtime versions from one codebase, with the original bulk-copy performance and write modes intact. This is the piece of work I would point to first: it started as one account's blocker and ended as shared infrastructure other engineering teams depend on.

Amazon Pay · Order transaction reporting · 2023–2024

Cut reporting latency from 36 hours to 30 minutes

Downstream teams were reporting on data that was up to a day and a half old. The batch extract was the bottleneck, and no amount of tuning was going to close a 36-hour gap — it needed a different shape.

36h → 30mReporting SLA
~99%Latency reduction
8 moLive with zero defects
0Production incidents

Problem

Order transaction data reached downstream consumers on a 36 to 40 hour SLA. Reporting was therefore always describing a state of the world that had already moved. The extract ran on a schedule, so latency was structural — a faster machine would not have helped.

Approach

I gathered the constraints from every team that consumed the data, evaluated several replication and streaming designs against them, and put forward two options: something we could ship within the quarter, and the architecture we should converge on. Batch became event-driven ingestion.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Stream at the source

    Database change streams pushed to object storage in the source account, near real time, so nothing sat waiting for a schedule.

  2. 2
    Replicate across accounts

    Cross-account, per-region replication of the encrypted payload, so the consuming platform never reached into the producing one.

  3. 3
    Transform on arrival

    An upload event triggered the transform rather than a timer — the pipeline moved when data moved, not when the clock did.

  4. 4
    Alarm on absence

    Failure alarms raised a ticket automatically, so a silent stall surfaced as an alert rather than as a question from a confused analyst.

What changed

Reporting SLA went from 36–40 hours to 30 minutes, a reduction of roughly 99%. More telling than the latency number: the system ran in production for eight months with zero defects. My manager put both figures in writing in my promotion document, which is the version I quote — I would rather use a number somebody else signed.
