01 / Approach

How I work, when nobody's watching

Make it boring on purpose

A pipeline that runs unattended for six months beats a clever one that needs a babysitter. I optimise for the 3am pager, not the design review.

Find the real question first

"Build me a dashboard" usually means "I don't trust this number." I go back one step and fix the number, which is often cheaper and always more useful.

Cost is a design constraint

Compute spend is somebody's headcount. I read the bill line by line and treat a 30% reduction as a shipped feature, because finance does.

Leave the map behind

Runbooks, lineage, and a README that assumes nothing. The test is whether a new joiner can debug my work without messaging me.

02 / Work

Three things worth explaining

Company One · 2023 — 2024 · Lead engineer, team of 3

Cut warehouse spend 38% without deleting a single dashboard

Finance flagged the data platform as the fastest-growing line item in engineering. The ask was "make it cheaper." The actual problem was that nobody knew what anything cost.

38%Lower monthly spend
4.2hNightly run, down from 11h
0Dashboards broken
100%Jobs with cost attribution

Problem

One shared cluster ran every job, so no team saw its own bill. Three pipelines were rebuilding full history nightly because that was easier than writing incremental logic two years earlier. Nobody would touch it — every table had an unknown consumer.

Approach

Measure before cutting. I tagged every job, built a lineage graph from query logs, and ranked tables by cost-per-read. That turned a political argument into a list. The bottom 20 tables had zero reads in 90 days.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Tag and attribute

    Job-level tagging pushed into billing exports, so every team saw its own number in a weekly digest.

  2. 2
    Build lineage from logs

    Parsed 90 days of query history into a table-to-consumer graph. Dead tables became provable, not debatable.

  3. 3
    Convert full loads to incremental

    Merge-on-read for the three worst offenders, with a weekly reconciliation job to catch drift.

  4. 4
    Split the cluster

    Separate compute for ELT, ad-hoc, and BI, each with its own autoscaling floor and a hard ceiling.

What changed

Spend dropped 38% in the first full month and stayed down, because the weekly digest made regressions visible to the team that caused them. The nightly window shrank from 11 hours to 4.2, which meant morning reports were ready before standup instead of during it. Two teams have since cut their own costs without involving me — which is the part I'm actually pleased about.

Company Two · 2024 — present · Design and delivery

Replaced a nightly batch with CDC, and stopped the 9am arguments

Ops and finance were reconciling against a snapshot that was up to 26 hours stale. Every morning began with two people disagreeing about a number that was simply out of date.

90sSource to serving lag
26hStaleness removed
1.4BRows migrated, zero loss
99.95%Pipeline uptime, 6 months

Problem

The nightly export locked the source database for 40 minutes and still missed late-arriving corrections. Downstream teams had built their own patches on top, so there were four versions of "revenue" in circulation.

Approach

Run both in parallel for six weeks and publish the diff daily. Trust had to be earned with evidence, not a migration plan. When the diff hit zero for fourteen consecutive days, the cutover was uncontroversial.

Architecture

  1. 1
    CDC at the source

    Log-based capture off Postgres replicas, so production write paths were untouched.

  2. 2
    Ordered, replayable transport

    Partitioned by entity key to preserve per-record ordering, with 7-day retention for replay after a bad deploy.

  3. 3
    Idempotent merge

    Upserts keyed on primary key plus source LSN, so a replay is a no-op rather than a duplication incident.

  4. 4
    Shadow and diff

    Old and new served side by side with an automated daily reconciliation report until the delta was flat.

What changed

Serving lag went from up to 26 hours to about 90 seconds, and the source database stopped taking a nightly hit. The four competing definitions of revenue collapsed into one, mostly because the fast number was also the correct one and the patches became pointless. The 9am reconciliation meeting was dropped from the calendar.

Company Two · 2025 · Solo, then handed over

Made bad data fail loudly instead of arriving quietly

The platform was reliable in the sense that jobs went green. It was unreliable in the sense that green jobs regularly shipped wrong numbers, and we found out from the business.

11 → 2Data incidents per quarter
4d → 35mTime to detect
240+Contract-checked columns
6Teams self-serving alerts

Problem

A silent upstream schema change had shifted a currency column two months earlier. Nobody noticed until a board deck showed a number that was obviously wrong, and then three engineers spent four days finding out why.

Approach

Tests where the data enters, not where it leaves. I resisted building a monitoring dashboard — dashboards need somebody to look at them. Failures had to reach a human in Slack, with the offending rows attached.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Contracts at the boundary

    Schema, nullability and range assertions declared in YAML next to each source, versioned in the same repo.

  2. 2
    Quarantine, don't drop

    Failing rows divert to a side table with the failed assertion attached, so nothing is lost and triage starts with evidence.

  3. 3
    Freshness and volume baselines

    Rolling expectations per table — a 40% row-count drop pages someone even when every assertion passes.

  4. 4
    Route to the owner

    Alerts go to the team that owns the source, not to a central channel where they go to die.

What changed

Detection time fell from around four days to 35 minutes, and quarterly data incidents dropped from eleven to two. The unexpected outcome was cultural: because alerts named an owner, six teams started adding their own contracts without being asked. I handed the whole thing over in a two-hour session and it has run without me since.
