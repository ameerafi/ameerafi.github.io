02 / Approach

How I work, when nobody's watching

Solve it once, then make it serve everyone

A connector fix for one customer became a maintained fork covering six runtimes that other teams now depend on. I look for the version of a fix that outlives the engagement, because the second customer with the same problem is always already out there.

Prove it before you ship it

I don't trust an optimisation measured on dev data, or a migration validated by sampling ten percent of rows. Two of my projects exist specifically because "probably fine" is not good enough when the blast radius is production.

Make safety structural, not behavioural

A sandbox that relies on people being careful will eventually meet somebody in a hurry. Fail-closed identity boundaries mean a stray redirect hits a permission wall instead of a production table — the guarantee holds even when the operator is wrong.

Leave it so someone else can own it

CI matrices, test suites, runbooks, and evidence stakeholders can validate without me in the room. The finish line is not deployment; it's the day the work no longer needs me.

Databricks · Enterprise accounts · FinOps

Took $180,000 a year out of enterprise Databricks workloads

Platform spend concentrated in a small set of high-DBU production jobs. The brief was to reduce it without changing a single business output.

$180K+Verified annual saving
50%Compute cut on one critical workload
4–5Of the top-20 costliest jobs
0Business outputs changed

Problem

Spend followed the usual shape — a long tail of cheap jobs and a short head of very expensive ones. The costly jobs were also the load-bearing ones, which is exactly why nobody had touched them. Any change had to be provably output-neutral before finance would count the saving.

Approach

Profile before touching anything, and make every change independently verifiable. I documented each optimisation with the evidence behind it so stakeholders could validate the result themselves — which is what turned these into verified savings rather than claimed ones.

What I changed

  1. 1
    Profile the execution plans

    DBU cost attribution per job, then read the Spark plans on the worst offenders rather than guessing from runtime alone.

  2. 2
    Prune the partitions

    Data layout was doing more damage than the SQL in several cases — partitioning that made sense when the table was small and stopped making sense years ago.

  3. 3
    Minimise the shuffle

    Restructured joins and removed redundant stages. The cheapest stage is the one that no longer runs.

  4. 4
    Evidence every change

    Before-and-after documented per job, so the saving was validated by the customer rather than asserted by me.

What changed

$180,000 a year in verified savings, including a 50% compute reduction on a single critical workload, with business output unchanged throughout. This is the piece of my work that translates most directly: deep Spark and Delta performance engineering, expressed as a number that appears on someone's budget.

Databricks · Lakebridge · Open-source contribution

Proved two billion-row tables match without moving the data

Every enterprise migrating onto Databricks has to prove the target matches the source. Row-by-row validation shuffles terabytes across the network, so at real scale teams give up, sample ten percent, and accept partial confidence instead of proof.

8.1×Faster validation
~48,000×Less network egress
15.8B+Rows verified
0False positives or negatives

Problem

Proving data equality after a migration traditionally means moving terabytes over the network for expensive row-by-row hash joins. At billion-row scale that is hours to days, or simply infeasible — so customers accept a risky ten percent sample. Sampling cannot prove equality; it can only fail to disprove it.

Approach

Stop comparing rows and start comparing mathematics. Compute compact algebraic aggregates where the data already lives, compare only the fingerprints, and when they differ, recover the exact differing rows by solving equations rather than searching for them.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Hash and partition

    Every row hashed to a fixed-width integer with cross-engine parity, then bucketed so source and target can be compared bucket by bucket.

  2. 2
    Aggregate at the source

    Sums, power sums and dual-channel collision hashes computed in the source database via JDBC pushdown — about 50 KB crosses the wire instead of gigabytes of rows.

  3. 3
    Compare and short-circuit

    Matching buckets resolve instantly and are never looked at again. On a clean migration almost everything resolves here.

  4. 4
    Solve for the differences

    An exact-difference solver recovers the differing rows from power-sum deltas, so a mismatch produces the offending records rather than just a red flag.

What changed

Migration sign-offs got 8.1× faster with ~48,000× less network egress, verified to 15.8 billion rows with zero false positives and zero false negatives across heterogeneous sources — Redshift, Teradata, Oracle and Snowflake. Built into Databricks' open-source Lakebridge migration tool, so it benefits every migration customer rather than one engagement. I also fixed three serialisation and timestamp-drift bugs in the Redshift connectors that had been quietly threatening data fidelity across all field migrations.

Databricks · Platform tooling · Optimisation safety

Proved a 10× speedup against 4.45 billion rows, with production untouched

Optimisations tested on small development datasets routinely fail or degrade under production volumes. Testing them in production risks data corruption and SLA breaches. Most teams pick one of those two bad options.

10.19×End-to-end acceleration proven
4.45B+Records verified identical
30+Pipeline families covered
0Production risk

Problem

Dev datasets are too small to show the skew, shuffle and file-scaling behaviour that decides whether an optimisation actually pays off. A Photon enablement that looks fine in dev can run slower in production, breach downstream SLAs, and have to be reverted. There was no systematic way to prove output stayed identical while performance improved.

Approach

Safety had to be structural rather than behavioural. A sandbox that depends on people being careful will eventually meet somebody in a hurry, so the identity boundary fails closed — a stray redirect hits a permission wall instead of a production table.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Discover the lineage

    Reads Unity Catalog lineage rather than parsing code, and fails loudly on empty lineage instead of quietly cloning nothing.

  2. 2
    Clone without copying

    Delta shallow clones give production-scale data with zero storage duplication — the reason testing at real volume is affordable at all.

  3. 3
    Redirect into isolation

    The job runs against the clone under a sandboxed identity, with unredirectable task types refusing to run rather than silently pointing at production.

  4. 4
    Prove parity, then judge performance

    Bidirectional EXCEPT ALL multiset verification catches added, dropped and duplicated rows plus schema drift. Cost attribution is deferred to its own step, because billing data lands hours later and a premature verdict is a misleading one.

What changed

A 10.19× end-to-end acceleration on a core business pipeline, proven safe by 100% byte-level parity across 4.45 billion records before it went anywhere near production. Teams could enable Photon and runtime upgrades with evidence instead of hope, across 30+ pipeline families, with no customer-facing code change required.

Databricks · AI governance · Runtime security

Stopped AI agents doing irreversible things to production

Autonomous agents now execute real production tasks — writing data, dropping tables, changing pipelines. Identity controls act before the action and log analysis acts after it. Nothing was checking the action itself, in flight.

6Inspection checkpoints per action
<30msPer-checkpoint latency
3Tiers of automated rollback
Art. 14EU AI Act audit evidence

Problem

An agent that can write to production can also destroy production. Traditional access control decides whether an identity may act; monitoring tells you afterwards what it did. Neither verifies that a specific action is correctly scoped and reversible at the moment it happens — which is the gap blocking enterprise agent adoption.

Approach

Put a gateway in the execution path and make every action prove itself before it lands. Crucially, capture enough state beforehand that anything which does get through can be reversed — governance that cannot undo is just observation with extra steps.

The six checkpoints

  1. 1
    Policy evaluation

    Global and team-level rules applied first, so plainly forbidden actions never reach the expensive checks.

  2. 2
    Scope boundary

    Blast radius per task — an agent asked to touch one table cannot quietly reach a hundred.

  3. 3
    Risk scoring

    Multi-factor score across weighted signals, which is what decides between allow, escalate and block.

  4. 4
    Human gate

    High-risk actions stop and wait for a person. The oversight requirement in the regulation is a human decision, not a log line.

  5. 5
    State snapshot

    Captured before execution, because rollback you did not prepare for is not rollback.

  6. 6
    Post-execution verification

    Confirms the action did what it claimed, and reverses it automatically via Delta time travel if it did not.

What changed

Enterprise teams get a way to run agents against production with visibility, control and reversibility rather than trust. Every action lands in an append-only, hash-chained audit ledger that stays tamper-evident even if the gateway itself is compromised — which is what turns it into compliance evidence for EU AI Act Article 14 and SOC 2 rather than just another log.

Databricks · Spark connector engineering

A fix for one customer that ended up serving everyone

Microsoft archived the Spark SQL Server connector. Customers needed a newer runtime for their Unity Catalog migrations, and the moment they upgraded, every write failed with a classpath error. There was no official migration path.

~15×Write throughput vs generic JDBC
14.3–18.xRuntime versions covered
1Codebase for all of them
0Private Spark APIs relied on

Problem

The connector's bulk-copy path was roughly fifteen times faster than generic JDBC, so it was load-bearing for high-volume writes. Once it was archived, upgrading the runtime broke an internal API signature and every write died. Migrations stopped dead, and there was no precedent to copy.

Approach

Root-cause it properly, then fix it so it cannot break the same way again. The revealing detail: the newer runtime forward-ports the next Spark major version's method signature while still reporting itself as the older version — so version detection was lying, and anything built on private APIs was going to keep breaking.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Trace the classpath

    Investigated across six runtime versions to find where the signature actually diverged, rather than trusting the reported version string.

  2. 2
    Unblock the migration

    A schema resolver compatible with both Spark 3.5 and 4.0, plus a reworked multi-profile build covering both Scala versions.

  3. 3
    Delegate to the public API

    The durable fix: a thin compatibility layer written entirely against Spark's stable public interfaces. No private types, therefore no per-version source branching — future upgrades become mechanical CI additions instead of risky refactors.

  4. 4
    Make it maintainable by anyone

    Automated regression gates with real SQL Server containers, so the next person to touch it does not need to have lived through the original diagnosis.

What changed

Unity Catalog migrations were unblocked with ~15× bulk write throughput preserved, and it spread on its own — colleagues asked for it to unblock their own customers. It became the standard maintained fork adopted across multiple engagements, covering six runtime versions from one codebase. This is the piece of work I would point to first: it started as one account's blocker and ended as shared infrastructure.

Amazon Pay · Payment data streams · Real-time architecture

Cut business reporting latency from 48 hours to 15 minutes

Downstream teams were reporting on payment data that was up to two days old. The batch extract was the bottleneck, and no amount of tuning was going to close a 48-hour gap — it needed a different shape.

48h → 15mReporting SLA
99%Latency reduction
400M+Transactions reconciled monthly
99.9%Pipeline uptime

Problem

Critical payment data reached downstream consumers on a 48-hour SLA, so reporting always described a state of the world that had already moved. The extract ran on a schedule, which made the latency structural — a faster machine would not have helped.

Approach

Gather the constraints from every consuming team, evaluate the options against them, and propose both something shippable this quarter and the architecture to converge on. Batch extraction became event-driven ingestion.

Architecture

  1. 1
    Land on object storage

    Source events written to S3 as they happen, so nothing waits for a schedule window to open.

  2. 2
    Trigger on arrival

    Lambda fires on the upload event rather than a timer — the pipeline moves when data moves, not when the clock does.

  3. 3
    Transform and catalogue

    Glue handles the transformation and keeps the catalogue current, so each file is queryable through Athena as soon as it lands.

  4. 4
    Alarm on absence

    CloudWatch metrics, log parsers and multi-tier alerting, so a silent stall surfaces as an alert rather than as a question from a confused analyst.

What changed

Reporting SLA fell from 48 hours to 15 minutes — a 99% reduction — on payment data streams feeding financial and leadership reporting. The same architecture carried 400M+ transactions a month across 15+ upstream services and 120+ ETL jobs, at 99.9% uptime with zero data loss. It turned a legacy batch estate into something the business could actually watch in near real time.
